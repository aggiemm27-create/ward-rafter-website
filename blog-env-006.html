The Wetlands Rules Are Gone. The Wetlands Law Isn't.

What an April Court Ruling Means for Property Owners

What Happened

In 2022, the Legislature rewrote New York's Freshwater Wetlands Act, Article 24 of the Environmental Conservation Law. For decades, DEC could regulate a freshwater wetland only if it appeared on an official state map. Starting January 1, 2025, that changed. The maps became informational, and any area that meets the statutory definition of a freshwater wetland is now presumed regulated whether or not anyone ever mapped it. The amendments also created 11 categories of smaller wetlands of "unusual importance" that can be regulated regardless of size. And they set the general size threshold to drop from 12.4 acres to 7.4 acres on January 1, 2028.

DEC put those changes into practice through new regulations at 6 NYCRR Part 664, which took effect the same day. Among other things, the rules placed wetlands in urban areas in Class II, a category where permits issue only in limited circumstances.

On April 8, 2026, in Chautauqua Lake Property Owners Association v. State of New York, Justice Richard Platkin of the Albany County Supreme Court annulled Part 664 in its entirety. The court held that DEC violated the State Environmental Quality Review Act. DEC had declared that the rules would cause no significant adverse environmental impact, on the premise that more wetland protection is always good for the environment. The court found that DEC never took a hard look at concerns raised during public comment, including the possibility that blocking development near wetlands would push growth somewhere else. In the court's words, there was "no reasoned elaboration" supporting DEC's conclusion.

What the Court Did Not Do

The decision is narrower than some coverage suggests. The court rejected the constitutional attacks on the 2022 statute itself, including due process, vagueness, improper delegation, and home rule claims. The amended Freshwater Wetlands Act is still the law. Only DEC's implementing regulations were struck down.

DEC has not pursued an appeal and instead says it will propose new regulations with public review and comment. Some of the challengers, led by the Chautauqua Lake Property Owners Association, have appealed to the Appellate Division, Third Department, asking that the statute itself be declared unconstitutional.

Where DEC Stands Today

Until new rules are in place, DEC has adopted an interim approach. Under its published guidance, DEC is asserting jurisdiction over two groups of wetlands: those that appeared on state maps before January 1, 2025, and those that are 12.4 acres or larger, mapped or not. The 100-foot regulated adjacent area around a regulated wetland still applies.

DEC says it is not currently asserting jurisdiction based solely on the "unusual importance" categories. That is an agency policy choice, not a change in the statute. Those categories remain in the law, and DEC has said its new regulations will restore additional wetland protections. A small unmapped wetland that falls outside DEC's reach today may not stay that way.

What Property Owners Should Do Now

Check any recent jurisdictional determination. If DEC issued you a positive jurisdictional determination between January 1, 2025 and April 8, 2026, look at what it relied on. DEC says determinations based on prior mapping or the 12.4-acre threshold remain valid. If yours rested only on the "unusual importance" criteria, DEC will reassess it on request through its online form.

Ask before you dig. If you plan to clear, fill, grade, or build near wet ground, request a jurisdictional determination first. DEC is still accepting and processing these requests. A written answer up front costs far less than an enforcement action after the fact.

Remember the other layers. This decision concerns state freshwater wetlands only. Tidal wetlands, which line much of Staten Island's shoreline, are governed by a separate statute. Federal permits from the Army Corps of Engineers may still be required.

The Bottom Line

The annulment gives some owners room to move, but it is temporary and incomplete. The statute stands, an appeal is pending, and new regulations are coming. Anyone who owns, buys, or develops land with wet areas on Staten Island or Long Island should confirm exactly where their property stands before starting work.
