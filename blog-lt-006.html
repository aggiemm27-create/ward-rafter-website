Inside the Rental Ripoff Report

NYC's New 23-Point Plan to Regulate Landlords

New York City landlords have a new document to add to their reading list. On July 16, 2026, Mayor Zohran Mamdani released the Rental Ripoff Report, a 68-page package of 23 policy actions aimed at tenant protections, housing quality, and landlord accountability. The report grew out of Rental Ripoff Hearings held in every borough between February and April, where more than 2,400 New Yorkers submitted testimony about their housing experiences.

The timing matters. The report landed just weeks after the Rent Guidelines Board voted to freeze rents on nearly one million stabilized apartments, and about a week before landlord groups filed suit challenging that freeze. Property owners are now tracking two fronts at once.

What Tenants Told the City

According to the report, heat and hot water complaints topped the list of 311 calls. Pests accounted for 16% of hearing testimony, mold another 13%, and leaks close behind at 13%. Tenants also described landlord harassment, confusing fee structures, and surprise utility charges. The city says this feedback shaped every recommendation in the report.

The Headline Proposals

A few items stand out for their scope. The administration wants formal legal recognition of tenant unions, giving organized tenant groups a defined role in building oversight and a structured way to communicate with owners. The report also calls for legislation banning credit checks in the rental application process and requiring disclosure whenever a rental listing photo has been altered using AI or other digital editing tools.

On the enforcement side, the city plans to stop treating multiple heat and hot water complaints from the same building as duplicates. Every individual complaint will get its own inspection starting this fall. Tenants will also be able to schedule certain inspections directly, similar to scheduling a delivery window.

Executive Actions Already in Motion

Some changes do not require legislation and can move through agency rulemaking or executive action alone. These include:

  • Investigating each heat complaint individually rather than bundling them by building
  • Letting tenants schedule select inspections
  • Speeding up response times for elevator outage complaints
  • Studying the root causes behind residential fires
  • Expanding protections against harassment tied to immigration status

What a Legislative Task Force Will Study

The Mayor's Office to Protect Tenants is also convening a task force to study longer-term reforms. Ideas on the table include financial penalties under the Underlying Conditions Program for landlords who do not remediate mold, a strengthened Alternative Enforcement Program for buildings with chronic violations, and modernizing property registration, which is still largely paper-based and does not require owners to list an email address. HPD could also gain the ability to serve violation notices digitally instead of relying solely on traditional service.

Not Everyone Is on Board

Landlord groups pushed back quickly. Some property owner representatives called the hearings a one-sided process that painted every landlord as a bad actor while ignoring financial pressure on rent-stabilized buildings, particularly after the rent freeze. New York Apartment Association CEO Kenny Burgos put it simply: a fine does not fix a building, and the city does not have the capacity to step in and manage rental housing at scale if private capital pulls back.

City officials counter that the report distinguishes between what they call "high-road" landlords and repeat offenders, and that the goal is targeted accountability rather than blanket punishment.

What Comes Next

Most of these 23 actions are recommendations, not law. Some can happen through agency rulemaking without City Council involvement. Others, like tenant union recognition and the credit check ban, need legislation and will move through the normal Council process, with room for negotiation, amendment, or delay along the way. Landlords should expect a mix of near-term operational changes and a longer runway of policy debate.

For now, no immediate compliance deadlines have been triggered by the report itself.
