A Court Struck Down New York's Section 8 Mandate

Why NYC Landlords Still Can't Act on It Yet

An upstate appellate court has ruled that New York cannot force landlords to accept Section 8 housing vouchers, calling the mandate an unconstitutional search. Headlines have framed the decision as the end of source-of-income protections in New York. That framing is premature. An automatic stay means the ruling has no legal effect yet, and landlords who act as if it does are taking on real risk.

What the Court Actually Decided

In March 2026, New York's Appellate Division, Third Department, issued a unanimous ruling in Matter of People of the State of New York v. Commons West, LLC that struck at the heart of the state's source-of-income protections. The case involved an upstate landlord who refused to accept Section 8 vouchers, arguing that New York's Human Rights Law could not force him to participate in the federal Section 8 program.

The court agreed. Participating in Section 8 requires a landlord to sign a Housing Assistance Payments contract, which gives government agencies the right to inspect the property and review business records without a warrant. The court found that conditioning a landlord's ability to rent housing on giving up that Fourth Amendment protection went too far. It marked the first time any appellate court in the country struck down a source-of-income discrimination law on constitutional grounds, and legal observers say the reasoning could reach similar laws in roughly nineteen other states.

Why This Doesn't Change Anything Yet

Here is the part that gets lost in the headlines. New York's Attorney General filed a notice of appeal to the Court of Appeals, the state's highest court, at the start of April. Under CPLR 5519(a)(1), that appeal triggered an automatic stay of the lower court's decision.

An automatic stay puts the ruling on hold. Nothing about landlords' legal obligations changed on the day the decision came down. The source-of-income protections in the New York State Human Rights Law remain fully enforceable while the case works its way through the Court of Appeals, which could take months or longer to reach a final decision.

Any landlord who reads a headline about vouchers being struck down and stops accepting Section 8 tenants on that basis is taking on real risk. The Division of Human Rights and other enforcement bodies are still accepting and pursuing source-of-income complaints. A landlord who refuses a voucher holder right now can still face a discrimination claim under the statute as it currently stands.

The NYC Wrinkle That Makes This Even More Complicated

For landlords in New York City, there is an added layer the upstate case does not touch. The Commons West decision addressed the state law passed as part of the 2019 Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act. New York City has had its own source-of-income protection under the NYC Human Rights Law since 2008, well before the state law existed, and it operates as a separate legal requirement.

Nothing in the Third Department's ruling reaches the city ordinance. Even if the state provision is eventually invalidated for good, NYC landlords, including those with rent-stabilized and market-rate buildings on Staten Island, would still be bound by the local law prohibiting refusal to rent based on how a tenant plans to pay. Two different legal regimes are in play here, and a ruling on one does not resolve the other.

What Comes Next

The Court of Appeals will eventually decide whether to uphold the Third Department's Fourth Amendment reasoning or reverse it. Given that the case has implications well beyond New York, and that housing advocates and landlord groups nationwide are watching closely, a reversal or a narrower ruling limited to the specific facts of the Ithaca case is entirely possible. Landlords should expect that this litigation will continue for some time before there is a final answer.

In the meantime, the safest course for any property owner is to treat both the state and city source-of-income protections as fully in effect. Screening policies, advertising language, and application procedures that reference voucher status should be reviewed with that in mind, not with an eye toward a ruling that has not taken legal effect and may never apply the way early coverage suggests.
