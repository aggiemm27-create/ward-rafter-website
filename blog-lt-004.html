Race-Blind Is Not Risk-Free

What New York's New Disparate Impact Law Means for Background Checks and Credit Minimums

Race-blind is not the same as risk-free. That is the core lesson of disparate impact law, and it is one that New York landlords can no longer afford to overlook. A tenant screening policy that never mentions race, national origin, or any other protected class can still violate fair housing law if it produces outcomes that fall disproportionately on a protected group. And as of December 2025, that standard is now written directly into New York State law.

What Disparate Impact Means

The Fair Housing Act has prohibited intentional housing discrimination since 1968. But the law reaches further than deliberate bias. In 2015, the Supreme Court confirmed in Texas Department of Housing and Community Affairs v. Inclusive Communities Project that the Act also covers policies that produce discriminatory outcomes, regardless of intent. This is the disparate impact standard. A landlord does not have to intend to discriminate. If a facially neutral policy produces results that fall more heavily on a protected class, and the landlord cannot justify it with a substantial business reason, the policy can be challenged as discriminatory.

New York Just Raised the Stakes

For years, disparate impact claims in housing were governed primarily by federal law and HUD regulations. That landscape is now shifting in two directions at once.

At the federal level, HUD proposed in January 2026 to eliminate its own disparate impact regulations, which have been in place since 2013. That proposal has not been finalized, and the Fair Housing Act itself still applies regardless of what happens to the regulation. But more importantly for landlords in this state, New York has moved to lock in the standard independently. In December 2025, Governor Hochul signed legislation amending the New York State Human Rights Law to explicitly allow housing discrimination claims based on a practice's discriminatory effect, even without proof of discriminatory intent. Whatever the federal government does next, New York landlords remain fully exposed under state law.

Where Screening Policies Get Landlords in Trouble

The two highest-risk areas are criminal background checks and credit score minimums.

A blanket policy rejecting any applicant with a criminal record looks neutral on its face. But because Black and Latino applicants are statistically more likely to have criminal records as a result of documented disparities in the criminal legal system, a no-exceptions policy can produce a disproportionate impact on those groups. HUD issued guidance in 2024 stating that individualized assessment is required rather than automatic rejection. The only categorical exclusion that consistently survives scrutiny is for applicants convicted of manufacturing or distributing a controlled substance.

Strict credit score cutoffs present similar exposure. Credit gaps are more common among people with disabilities, applicants who rely on public assistance, and housing voucher holders. A rigid minimum that functions as a proxy for income or background can draw a fair housing challenge even when the landlord had no discriminatory intent whatsoever.

What a Defensible Screening Policy Looks Like

Disparate impact liability does not mean landlords cannot screen tenants. It means the screening has to hold up to scrutiny.

A policy survives a disparate impact challenge if it is supported by a substantial, legitimate, nondiscriminatory interest and no less discriminatory alternative would serve that interest equally well. In practice, that means individualized review rather than automatic disqualification. For criminal records, courts and regulators look at the nature of the offense, the time elapsed, and how relevant the conduct is to housing tenancy. For credit, landlords who consider the full picture, including rental history, income verification, and references, are better positioned than those applying a hard numerical cutoff.

Documentation matters too. A written, consistently applied screening policy is harder to challenge than informal decision-making that varies applicant to applicant.

The Bottom Line for New York Landlords

The federal regulatory picture on disparate impact may be in flux, but New York's position is not. The state's December 2025 law means that landlords operating here face potential liability under state human rights law even if federal protections are rolled back. A screening policy that was never designed to discriminate can still produce a discrimination claim if its effects are uneven and its justification is weak.
