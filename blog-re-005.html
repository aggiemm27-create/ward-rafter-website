How an Ignored Encroachment Can Become a Legal Right in New York

An encroachment happens when a structure, fence, driveway, or planting crosses from one property onto another. Most encroachments start small. A fence installed a few feet off the actual boundary line. A shed built too close to the property edge. A paved section of driveway that drifts onto the neighboring lot. None of this transfers ownership by itself. The property line stays where the deed and survey say it is.

Where Adverse Possession Comes In

New York law allows a person to eventually gain legal title to land that belongs to someone else, if certain conditions are met over a long enough period. This is called adverse possession. Under New York's statute, a claimant generally must show possession that is actual, open and notorious, exclusive, continuous, and hostile to the true owner's interest, for a period of ten years.

Open and notorious means the use has to be visible enough that a reasonable property owner would notice it. Continuous means the possession cannot be interrupted for any meaningful stretch of time. Hostile does not require ill will. It simply means the person using the land is not doing so with the true owner's permission.

A fence, a shed, or a section of pavement that sits on a neighbor's land for a decade without objection can meet these requirements. When that happens, a court can recognize the encroaching party as the legal owner of that strip of land, even though the original deed says otherwise.

Prescriptive Easements Work Similarly

A related concept is the prescriptive easement. Instead of gaining ownership outright, a neighbor can gain a permanent legal right to use a portion of another person's property, such as a shared driveway or a walking path, if that use has continued openly for the statutory period. The property owner keeps title, but loses the ability to stop the use.

Why Waiting Is the Real Risk

The biggest mistake a property owner can make with an encroachment is doing nothing. Every year that passes without objection moves the encroaching party closer to a legal claim. Once ten years pass, the property owner's options narrow considerably, and what could have been resolved with a letter or a short negotiation can turn into a contested court case over legal title.

Documenting the encroachment early matters. A current survey showing the boundary line and the encroaching structure is often the starting point. A written objection or demand for removal, sent before the statutory period runs, helps preserve the property owner's rights and can interrupt a claim of continuous, uninterrupted use.

What This Means for Property Owners

Anyone who owns real estate in New York, whether a single-family home, a rental property, or vacant land, should periodically check the physical boundaries against the recorded survey. A fence or structure that looks minor today can carry significant legal weight after enough time passes without a challenge.

Buyers should pay close attention to this issue as well. A title search alone will not always reveal a boundary encroachment. A current survey, reviewed before closing, is often the only way to catch it before it becomes the buyer's problem.
