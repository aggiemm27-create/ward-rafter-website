When "Sole Discretion" Isn't Absolute

What a New Court of Appeals Ruling Means for New York Contracts

On May 28, 2026, the New York Court of Appeals issued a decision that commercial litigators and transactional lawyers alike have been talking about ever since. In a 4-3 split, the court held in 111 West 57th Investment LLC v. 111 W57 Mezz Investor LLC that a party given "sole discretion" under a contract can still be found to have breached the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing if that discretion was exercised in bad faith.

For businesses of every size, this matters. Countless commercial contracts in New York give one party discretionary rights: the right to approve or reject a transfer, the right to assign an interest, the right to sign off on a decision. This ruling puts a real limit on how far those rights can be exercised.

What Happened in the Case

The dispute arose from a $65 million equity investment in a Manhattan luxury residential tower. The plaintiff invested alongside other parties in the project. When the deal ran into trouble, one of the lenders assigned a junior mezzanine loan as part of what the plaintiff alleged was a "backroom deal," an arrangement that allowed the assigning party and others to benefit from a strict foreclosure at the plaintiff's expense.

The lender argued it had done nothing wrong because the loan agreement gave it absolute discretion to assign the loan to whomever it chose. Lower courts agreed, reasoning that a party cannot breach an implied duty of good faith when a contract expressly grants unrestricted discretion. The Court of Appeals disagreed and reinstated the plaintiff's claim.

The Court's Reasoning

Every contract in New York carries an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. The majority held that this covenant survives even where a party has been given sole or absolute discretion over a particular decision. Discretion granted by contract, the court explained, is not a blank check to act in bad faith or for an improper purpose. A party can still exercise its discretionary rights aggressively and in its own self-interest. What it cannot do, under this ruling, is use that discretion as a tool to intentionally harm another party to the deal through concealment or collusion.

The dissent took a different view, warning that this approach injects uncertainty into commercial contracts that were negotiated by sophisticated parties who specifically bargained for unrestricted discretion. According to the dissent, courts have historically given wide berth to freedom of contract in these situations, and this ruling risks inviting more litigation any time a discretionary decision produces an unhappy outcome for the other side.

Where the Line Now Sits

The distinction the majority draws is between a party exercising discretion for its own legitimate business reasons, even harsh ones, and a party using that discretion as cover for something else entirely. Simply making a decision that benefits one side over another is not enough to state a claim. What moved the needle in this case were specific allegations of a coordinated arrangement designed to funnel value away from the plaintiff. Businesses should not read this ruling as inviting a challenge to every unfavorable discretionary decision. The bar for pleading bad faith remains meaningfully higher than simply being unhappy with an outcome.

What This Means Going Forward

This decision does not eliminate discretionary contract clauses or make them meaningless. Courts will still generally defer to how a party exercises discretion it was given. But businesses drafting or relying on these clauses should understand that "sole discretion" language, on its own, is no longer an automatic shield against a good faith claim if the underlying conduct suggests bad faith, collusion, or an intent to disadvantage a counterparty.

For businesses entering into joint ventures, financing agreements, partnership arrangements, or any contract where one party holds approval or assignment rights over the other, this ruling is worth understanding before problems arise, not after. How courts apply this standard in the months ahead, and whether it produces the wave of litigation the dissent predicted, remains an open question.
