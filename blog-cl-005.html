The Case That Almost Ended at Summary Judgment

M.V.B. Collision, doing business as Mid Island Collision, repaired a vehicle owned by a State Farm policyholder named Jerone Sealy. Sealy assigned his rights under the policy to the shop, a common practice in the collision repair industry that lets the business pursue payment directly instead of waiting on the customer. The shop sued State Farm for breach of contract, claiming the insurer never paid the full cost of the repairs and ignored state regulations governing how those payments are calculated.

A Nassau County trial court sided with State Farm and threw the case out on summary judgment in 2023. On May 27, 2026, the Appellate Division, Second Department, reversed that decision and sent the case back. The shop gets its day in court after all.

Why the Trial Court's Decision Didn't Hold Up

An insurance policy is a contract. That sounds obvious, but it matters because it means an insurer that fails to pay what a policy requires can be sued the same way any other business gets sued for breaking a deal. To win a breach of contract claim in New York, a plaintiff has to show four things: a contract existed, the plaintiff held up its end, the defendant failed to hold up its end, and that failure caused real damages.

The fight here came down to whether State Farm paid what it was supposed to pay. Under New York regulations, insurers have to pay the prevailing competitive price for labor when a vehicle gets repaired. State Farm argued its payment was adequate. The appellate court disagreed that this question was settled. The insurer's own evidence left open real factual disputes about whether it paid the going labor rate and whether its final repair estimate covered what the vehicle actually needed. When there is a genuine dispute like that, a case cannot be thrown out before trial. The insurer has to prove there is nothing left to argue about, and here it did not clear that bar.

The Part Every Business Owner Should Notice

Here is the detail that reaches beyond auto body shops. The regulation at issue, found in the state's insurance regulations, was not written into the State Farm policy word for word. It did not need to be. New York law treats applicable insurance regulations as if they are printed directly into the contract, whether the insurer includes that language or not. A business relying only on the four corners of a written agreement can miss obligations that exist regardless of what the paper says.

That principle is not unique to insurance. Contracts across many industries operate against a backdrop of state and federal rules that shape what performance actually requires. A vendor agreement, a service contract, or a supply deal can carry implied obligations tied to licensing rules, safety codes, or industry-specific regulations, even when the contract itself is silent. Businesses that assume a signed agreement is the whole story sometimes find out otherwise in litigation.

What This Means Going Forward

This decision does not decide who wins the underlying dispute. It only means the case gets to proceed past the point where a defendant tried to end it early. That distinction matters. Surviving summary judgment is not a verdict. It is a determination that the facts are genuinely disputed enough to require a trial or a settlement, rather than a dismissal on paper.

For collision shops and other businesses that regularly deal with insurers or large counterparties, the case is a reminder that contract disputes are not always won or lost on the four corners of the agreement. Regulatory requirements, industry standards, and prior dealings between the parties can all become part of what a court looks at when deciding whether a contract was actually honored.
