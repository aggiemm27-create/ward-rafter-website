A Body Shop's Insurance Fight Just Got a Second Life

What a New Appellate Ruling Means for Insurance Disputes in New York

Auto body shops in New York have spent years pushing back against insurers that pay less than what repairs actually cost. A new decision from the Appellate Division, Second Department gives that fight fresh momentum, and it is worth understanding whether you run a repair shop, a small business with a commercial insurance dispute, or any company that relies on an insurer to honor its policy terms.

What Happened in M.V.B. Collision v. State Farm

The case involved a Long Island collision shop, doing business as Mid Island Collision, that repaired a customer's vehicle and took an assignment of the customer's insurance claim against State Farm. The shop sued State Farm for breach of contract, arguing the insurer never paid the full, legally required cost of the repairs.

State Farm moved for summary judgment in the trial court and won. The shop appealed. In May 2026, the Second Department reversed that decision and sent the case back for trial.

The Rule That Mattered: Prevailing Competitive Price

New York insurance regulations require that when an insurer pays for covered vehicle repairs, the payment must reflect the prevailing competitive price for labor and parts in that market. This standard comes from 11 NYCRR 216.7, part of the regulatory framework that governs how auto insurers handle first-party and assigned claims.

The appellate court found that State Farm's own submissions did not eliminate the factual dispute over two separate questions: whether the insurer actually paid the prevailing competitive labor rate, and whether its final supplemental estimate was even enough to complete the repair. Because those questions remained genuinely disputed, the court held that summary judgment should never have been granted in State Farm's favor. The case is going back for a trial on the merits.

Why This Matters Beyond One Body Shop

This decision does not create new law so much as it reinforces an existing one: insurers cannot simply assert that their payment satisfied the regulatory standard and expect a court to take that on faith. Basic breach of contract principles still apply. A plaintiff has to show a contract existed, that it performed its side, that the defendant breached its obligations, and that damages resulted. Here, the shop's evidence was enough to at least get past summary judgment and put those questions in front of a jury.

For any business that has taken an assignment of an insurance claim, whether in the collision repair industry or elsewhere, this case is a useful data point. It shows that a court will look closely at whether an insurer's estimate and payment history actually track the regulatory pricing standard, rather than accepting the insurer's own numbers as conclusive.

What This Means Going Forward

If you run a repair shop or another business that regularly deals with insurance company payouts, a denial or a lowball estimate is not necessarily the end of the conversation. Courts are willing to scrutinize whether an insurer's payment reflects what the regulations actually require, and an insurer's summary judgment motion does not automatically end the case just because it says its numbers were fair.

Commercial litigation involving insurance disputes often comes down to exactly this kind of technical, fact-intensive question. Whether the dispute involves a breach of contract claim, an underpayment on a covered loss, or a broader disagreement about how an insurer is handling its obligations under New York law, the details of the regulatory standard can make or break the case.
