New York Just Expanded GBL 349

But Only the Attorney General Can Use the New Teeth

New York General Business Law Section 349 has been the state's primary tool against deceptive business practices for decades, and it shows up constantly in commercial litigation involving insurance disputes, vendor conduct, and consumer-facing business practices. As of February 17, 2026, the statute covers more ground. Businesses operating in New York should understand what changed and, just as importantly, who can actually enforce it.

What Changed

Since 1970, GBL 349 has prohibited "deceptive acts or practices" in the conduct of business. That standard requires a plaintiff to show the challenged conduct was consumer-oriented, materially misleading, and caused an actual injury. Courts have applied it broadly over the years, reaching everything from insurance claim handling to false advertising to misleading loan terms.

The February 2026 amendment adds a new category alongside "deceptive": conduct that is "unfair and abusive." This tracks language used in federal consumer financial protection law, and it is generally understood to reach a wider range of conduct than deception alone. An act can be unfair or abusive without necessarily involving a false or misleading statement. Practices that exploit a consumer's lack of understanding, take unreasonable advantage of a power imbalance, or impose substantial injury a consumer could not reasonably avoid may now fall within the statute even where no one told an outright lie.

Why This Distinction Matters More Than It Sounds

Here is the part businesses need to pay close attention to: the new "unfair and abusive" language is enforceable only by the New York Attorney General. Private plaintiffs, including businesses that bring GBL 349 claims against competitors, vendors, or counterparties, still have to rely on the traditional deceptive-acts standard to bring their own lawsuits. A private litigant cannot sue simply because conduct was unfair or abusive under the new language. They still need to plead and prove the original three elements: consumer-oriented conduct, material misrepresentation, and resulting injury.

That distinction is easy to miss because both provisions now live in the same statute. Businesses reading headlines about an "expanded" GBL 349 might assume their own exposure to private lawsuits has grown across the board. It has not, at least not directly. What has grown is the Attorney General's toolkit for pursuing enforcement actions against businesses whose practices the private right of action was never designed to reach.

What This Means for New York Businesses

For a business that already has to think about GBL 349 exposure, whether because of how it markets to consumers, structures fees, or handles disputes, this amendment is worth building into a compliance review even though it does not change the litigation risk from private plaintiffs. A business practice that survives scrutiny under the traditional deceptive-acts test is not automatically safe from an AG inquiry under the new unfair-and-abusive standard, since that standard does not require the same showing of a misleading statement.

For businesses on the other side, such as those relying on GBL 349 to pursue a private claim against an insurer, vendor, or counterparty, the private right of action framework has not gotten any easier or harder. The traditional three-part test still controls, and the practical lesson from existing case law, including cases involving underpayment by insurers, still applies: consumer-oriented conduct connected to a pattern of misleading representations remains the strongest foundation for a private claim.

Businesses should treat the amendment as a signal that state regulators have a broader mandate going forward, not as a new avenue for private litigation. Understanding which lane a given dispute falls into, private right of action or AG enforcement authority, is the first step in evaluating exposure or building a claim under the amended statute.
