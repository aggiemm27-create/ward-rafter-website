The Case: ARLO 67, LLC v. Doyle

ARLO 67, LLC brought a partition action over a residential property in Kings County Supreme Court. In February 2023, the court declared ARLO a 75% owner and directed that the property be sold at auction. ARLO bought it at that auction. According to the appellate court, it is not clear whether the closing ever happened.

Then the Attorney General's office stepped in. It was investigating possible deed theft involving the property, and it moved to stay the case under RPAPL 756-a. The Kings County Supreme Court effectively denied the motion in February 2025.

On August 5, 2026, the Appellate Division, Second Department, reversed. It granted the Attorney General's motion and stayed all proceedings in the case, including any sale of the property, for six months.

The Law Behind the Stay

RPAPL 756-a was added in 2024 as part of New York's response to the deed theft crisis. It gives federal, state, and local government agencies a tool to pause civil cases while they investigate whether a property's title or financing was obtained through theft or fraud.

The statute covers more than eviction cases. It reaches ejectment actions, foreclosures, actions to enforce a mortgage note, and any other action affecting title to real property. Partition falls within that category.

The key language is mandatory. If an agency shows that "a good faith investigation" is pending, the court "shall issue a stay." The stay can continue as long as the investigation is ongoing, with the court holding a status conference every six months. The court may review the agency's supporting documents privately, and the agency can ask that they be filed under seal.

How Low the Bar Is

Because no appellate court had interpreted the "good faith investigation" requirement before, the Second Department had to define it. It concluded the requirement "is not meant to be onerous for the investigating agency."

Under the court's standard, the agency does not have to prove fraud. It does not even have to show probable cause. The factual basis is enough "as long as the futility of the process is not inevitable or obvious." The agency must show that its investigative steps bear a reasonable relation to the subject of the investigation and to the public purpose behind it. And the basis has to be more than isolated or rare complaints.

The court also held that agencies do not get an automatic presumption of good faith here. The statute puts the burden on the agency to make its showing. But once it does, the court has no discretion to refuse the stay.

The Detail That Stands Out

The person said to be the victim of the deed theft denied having been defrauded. The lower court gave that significant weight. The appellate court did not. It found the Attorney General still had a good-faith basis to investigate and was still trying to interview ARLO's sole owner, which was reasonably related to the inquiry.

In other words, an alleged victim's denial does not end the matter. The investigation, and the stay, can go forward anyway.

What This Means for Property Litigation

For parties on the investigation side, the decision confirms that courts will act quickly to keep a disputed property from changing hands while the government looks into it. That protects homeowners whose title may have been stolen and who may not even know a case is pending.

For investors, lenders, and buyers, it means a case can stall at any stage, even after judgment and after an auction. A partition sale, a foreclosure, or a quiet title action involving residential property can be put on hold based on an investigation the other side may never fully see. Six-month review cycles can turn into a much longer delay.

Anyone acquiring an interest in residential property through litigation, or buying at a court-ordered sale, should expect that the title history may draw scrutiny well after the case seems to be over.
