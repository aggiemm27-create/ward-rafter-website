Believing It's Yours Isn't Enough

A Staten Island Case Shows What Adverse Possession Actually Requires

A Staten Island property dispute reached the Appellate Division this spring, and the plaintiffs lost their adverse possession claim before the case ever got to trial. The reason why offers a real lesson in how New York courts scrutinize these claims, and how a poorly drafted pleading can sink a case on its own.

What Happened in Henry v. Khan

In 2020, Ehsan Khan acquired title to a piece of real property on Staten Island. Three years later, a group of plaintiffs sued him under Article 15 of the Real Property Actions and Proceedings Law, a statute commonly used to resolve competing claims to land. Their theory was adverse possession. They argued that despite Khan holding the deed, they were the true owners because of how they had used the property over time.

Khan moved for summary judgment, asking the court to throw out the case and declare that the plaintiffs had no ownership interest. The plaintiffs, in turn, asked for permission to amend their complaint. The Supreme Court, Richmond County, sided with Khan on both fronts. It dismissed the case and denied the plaintiffs' request to amend. The plaintiffs appealed to the Appellate Division, Second Department, which affirmed the dismissal this past April.

The Legal Standard for Adverse Possession in New York

Adverse possession lets someone acquire legal title to land they don't formally own, but only if they can prove five specific things: their possession was hostile and under a claim of right, actual, open and notorious, exclusive, and continuous for the statutory period.

"Claim of right" is the piece that ended this case. New York substantially rewrote its adverse possession law in 2008, in part to stop people from claiming land they knew belonged to someone else. Under the current statute, a claim of right means the possessor had a reasonable basis for believing the property was actually theirs. It's not enough to have used the land. The person has to have genuinely believed they owned it.

Why the Plaintiffs' Own Complaint Doomed Their Case

Here's the detail that makes this case worth paying attention to. The plaintiffs didn't just lose on weak evidence. Their own proposed amended complaint contained facts that contradicted the claim of right element. In other words, in trying to strengthen their case by amending it, they ended up pleading themselves out of it. The court found that their new allegations affirmatively showed they lacked a reasonable basis for believing the property belonged to them, which meant there was no version of the case, as pled, that could support an adverse possession claim.

That is why the court didn't just deny the plaintiffs' motion to amend. It went a step further and directed that judgment be entered declaring that the plaintiffs never acquired title by adverse possession at all. A weak claim gets dismissed. A claim that defeats itself in its own pleadings gets shut down entirely, with a formal declaration on the record.

What This Case Signals for Property Owners

Quiet title actions under RPAPL Article 15 are one of the more common tools in New York real estate litigation. They exist to resolve exactly this kind of dispute, where one party holds record title and another claims a competing interest based on long-term use or possession. Staten Island, with its mix of older housing stock, informal boundary arrangements, and multi-generational property use, sees its share of these disputes.

Henry v. Khan is a reminder that adverse possession is not simply about how long someone has used a piece of land. Courts look closely at the state of mind behind that use. A possessor who knew, or who admits facts showing they should have known, that the land belonged to someone else does not meet the legal standard, no matter how long the use continued. The case also shows how the specific wording of a pleading can carry real legal weight. Facts included to support one argument can end up defeating another.

For property owners on either side of a boundary or possession dispute, whether defending record title or asserting a claim based on years of use, these cases turn on details that are easy to underestimate before litigation begins.
