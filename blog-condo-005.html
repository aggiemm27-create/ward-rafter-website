The Selective Enforcement Trap

When Inconsistent Rule Enforcement Becomes a Racial Discrimination Claim

Most condo and HOA boards understand that they cannot reject a buyer because of race. What far fewer boards understand is that discrimination claims rarely start with an outright rejection. They start with a noise complaint that got ignored. A fine that got waived for one owner but not another. A repair request that sat unanswered for months while similar requests in other units were handled promptly. Selective enforcement of the rules is one of the most common sources of fair housing liability for New York boards, and one of the most underestimated.

How Selective Enforcement Becomes a Legal Problem

Boards derive their authority from the governing documents: the bylaws, house rules, and declaration. That authority comes with an obligation to exercise it consistently. When a board enforces rules against some unit owners but not others, it opens the door to a claim that the inconsistency was motivated by something other than the rules themselves.

Under the Fair Housing Act, the New York State Human Rights Law, and the New York City Human Rights Law, housing discrimination based on race, color, national origin, and other protected characteristics is prohibited. That prohibition extends beyond the denial of housing. It covers the full range of terms, conditions, and privileges of ownership, which courts have read broadly to include how a board treats owners after they are already in the building.

New York's Division of Human Rights has found probable cause in cases where a board's pattern of inconsistent rule enforcement was alleged to reflect racial bias. A board does not have to announce discriminatory intent. A pattern of decisions is enough to raise the question.

Where Boards Tend to Get Into Trouble

Selective enforcement claims cluster around a few recurring situations.

Noise and nuisance complaints are near the top of the list. When a board responds quickly to complaints about one unit while repeatedly failing to act on identical complaints from another, the disparity becomes evidence. The same applies to alteration and renovation approvals. A board that routinely grants approval for some owners while imposing delays, extra requirements, or denials on others without a documented and consistent rationale is building a record that a fair housing attorney will know how to use.

Fines are another pressure point. A board that imposes fines for violations in one unit while overlooking the same violations next door is not just exposed under the business judgment rule. It is also exposed to a discrimination claim if the pattern of selective fining tracks along protected class lines.

Common area access and amenity use have generated claims as well. Guest policies, pool rules, and parking restrictions that are enforced inconsistently are vulnerable to challenge, particularly when the owners who receive less favorable treatment belong to a protected class.

What the Damages Look Like

This is where boards tend to underestimate their exposure. A successful fair housing claim is not just an injunction telling the board to stop what it is doing. The remedies available under federal and state law are substantial.

Under the Fair Housing Act, a prevailing plaintiff can recover compensatory damages for emotional distress and other actual harm, punitive damages in cases where the conduct was willful or reckless, and attorney's fees. Punitive damages in federal fair housing cases have reached six figures in cases involving repeated or egregious conduct. Under the New York City Human Rights Law, which is interpreted more broadly than its federal counterpart, there is no cap on compensatory or punitive damages, and the prevailing party standard for attorney's fees favors plaintiffs.

Complaints filed with the New York State Division of Human Rights or the NYC Commission on Human Rights can result in civil penalties on top of damages.

What Builds a Case

A single instance of unequal treatment rarely wins a fair housing case on its own. What builds a case is a pattern. Plaintiffs and their attorneys look for documentation showing how the board handled similar situations with different owners: meeting minutes, correspondence, fine notices, repair logs, and approval records.

This is why the board's own records are often its best defense or its worst liability. A board that documents its decisions and applies its standards in writing has something to point to when a claim arises. A board that operates informally, makes exceptions without explanation, and keeps sparse records has no paper trail to contradict a pattern allegation.

The December 2025 Shift in New York Law

New York's legal landscape on discrimination changed at the end of 2025. Governor Hochul signed legislation amending the New York State Human Rights Law to codify the disparate impact standard. A board policy or practice can now be challenged based on its discriminatory effect alone, without proof of discriminatory intent. That change makes selective enforcement claims easier to bring. A consistent pattern of unequal outcomes may be enough to support a claim even where the board had no conscious bias.

What Consistent Enforcement Actually Looks Like

Consistency is the foundation of any defensible position. Boards should apply their governing documents the same way for every owner, document the basis for their decisions, and review how they have handled similar situations before acting on a new one.

When a board is about to make an exception, waiving a fine, expediting an approval, or declining to pursue a complaint, that decision should be documented. The rationale should be one that could be applied equally to any other owner in the same situation.

When the facts are complicated or the pattern of past decisions is inconsistent, involving legal counsel before taking action is far less costly than responding to a complaint after the fact.
