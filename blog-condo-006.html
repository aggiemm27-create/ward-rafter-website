The Six-Year Clock on Common Charge Liens

What New York Condo and HOA Boards Should Know About Lien Duration and Debt Recovery

New York law gives condominium and homeowners association boards a lien against a unit or property for unpaid common charges or assessments. That lien secures the amount owed and allows a board to pursue foreclosure, in the same manner as a mortgage. For most associations, the lien has a limited life. It generally expires six years from the date it was filed.

Boards that track only the total dollar figure owed on a unit can miss what that six-year window actually means for their ability to collect.

The Lien Has a Shelf Life, But the Debt Might Not

Once six years pass from the filing date, the recorded lien can no longer be foreclosed. The board loses that specific enforcement tool for the amount the lien secured. That does not mean the debt disappears. The recorded lien stays on file at the county clerk's office. It still shows up as a cloud on title, and buyers, lenders, and title companies handling a future sale or refinance often require it to be addressed before closing, even after the foreclosure window has technically closed.

An association also has the option to sue for a money judgment for unpaid common charges, separate from lien foreclosure. That claim runs on its own six-year clock under New York contract law, and courts have clarified how that clock actually works for a recurring debt like common charges.

Each Month Gets Its Own Six Years

Common charges and assessments accrue monthly. Courts have held that each month's charge is treated as a separate obligation for statute of limitations purposes, not as one lump debt that becomes time-barred all at once. Under that approach, a board can generally still recover charges going back six years from when it files an action, even where the full arrears history goes back much further. Anything older than that six-year window falls outside what the action can reach.

Practically, this means a board is never facing a single hard cutoff where an entire arrears balance becomes uncollectible overnight. The oldest unpaid months age out of the six-year window one at a time, while newly accrued charges start their own fresh six-year period.

What This Means for Ongoing Collection Efforts

Because of how the clock runs, boards handling long-standing delinquencies typically need an ongoing collection strategy rather than a single lien filing. Common approaches include filing an updated notice of lien to capture newly accrued charges as an older lien approaches expiration, and pursuing a money judgment before the oldest portion of an arrears balance ages past six years.

A money judgment carries different, and generally longer, time limits than the underlying common charge lien. Once entered, a judgment is enforceable for twenty years under CPLR 211(b). When docketed with the county clerk, it also creates its own lien against the debtor's real property, which lasts ten years and can be renewed for an additional ten years under CPLR 5014. That gives an association a considerably longer runway to collect than the original six-year common charge lien alone provides.

The Bottom Line

Lien duration, per-installment accrual, and judgment enforcement periods each run on different timelines under New York law. Boards managing long-outstanding common charge arrears are working against more than one deadline at a time.
