The Contract You Never Got

How Minority Business Owners Can Fight Back When Race Costs Them the Deal

You submitted the bid. You had the qualifications. You had the price. And then the contract went somewhere else, with no real explanation. For minority business owners in New York, that experience is not rare. A January 2026 report from the NYC Comptroller found that minority and women-owned businesses receive just 5% of city procurement contracts, despite programs specifically designed to expand their access. When the numbers are that lopsided and the explanations do not add up, the question worth asking is whether race played a role and whether the law gives you a way to fight back.

It does.

A Federal Law Most Business Owners Have Never Heard Of

Most people associate discrimination claims with employment. But a federal statute that dates to the Civil Rights Act of 1866, known as 42 U.S.C. Section 1981, protects the right of every person to make and enforce contracts on equal terms regardless of race. That protection extends far beyond the employer-employee relationship. It covers vendors, subcontractors, independent contractors, and business owners of every kind.

Section 1981 claims have been brought by minority-owned businesses that were denied contracts despite being the most qualified bidder, removed from projects mid-stream after the client learned the owner's race, offered worse pricing or terms than non-minority competitors, or cut off from negotiations without explanation after a relationship that had been moving forward. The law reaches all of these situations. It also does not require you to first file a complaint with the EEOC or any other administrative agency. You can go straight to federal court.

What Makes Section 1981 Different From Other Discrimination Laws

Section 1981 has several features that make it particularly powerful for business owners pursuing commercial discrimination claims.

There is no minimum size requirement for the defendant. Title VII, the better-known federal anti-discrimination law, only applies to employers with 15 or more employees. Section 1981 applies to any private party regardless of size. A sole proprietor, a small vendor, or a large corporation can all be held liable.

The statute of limitations is four years. That is longer than most employment discrimination claims and gives business owners more time to identify a pattern and build a case.

There is no cap on damages. A successful Section 1981 plaintiff can recover compensatory damages, lost profits, and punitive damages in cases where the defendant's conduct was willful or reckless. In commercial cases involving significant contracts, that exposure can be substantial.

Individual defendants can also be named. The person who made the discriminatory decision, not just the company they work for, can face personal liability under Section 1981.

What You Have to Prove

Section 1981 requires proof of intentional discrimination. Unlike some other civil rights frameworks, it does not allow claims based solely on disparate impact. You cannot win just by showing that a policy produced unequal outcomes. You have to show that race was a but-for cause of the decision that harmed you.

That is a real burden, but it is not an impossible one. Courts have found intentional discrimination based on circumstantial evidence, including patterns of how a company treats minority-owned businesses compared to similarly situated non-minority competitors, communications that reveal bias, suspicious timing in contract terminations, and testimony from others who experienced similar treatment.

The Second Circuit, which covers New York federal courts, has recognized Section 1981 claims in commercial contracting contexts. The legal framework is established. What varies is the strength of the facts in each case.

New York State Law Adds Another Layer

Section 1981 is not the only tool available. The New York State Human Rights Law and the New York City Human Rights Law also prohibit race discrimination in commercial contracting contexts. The NYCHRL in particular is interpreted broadly in favor of plaintiffs and imposes no cap on compensatory or punitive damages. In appropriate cases, claims under state and city law can be brought alongside a Section 1981 claim, giving a plaintiff multiple legal theories and multiple avenues for recovery.

When to Take the Pattern Seriously

Not every lost contract is a discrimination case. But when a minority business owner consistently loses opportunities to less-qualified competitors, is removed from projects after race becomes apparent, receives pretextual explanations that do not hold up under scrutiny, or notices that non-minority businesses in the same space are treated differently, those facts are worth putting in front of an attorney who handles civil rights and commercial litigation.
