Blaming the Board

Why Tortious Interference Claims Against Condo and HOA Boards Rarely Succeed

A property owner lines up a buyer. The deal is moving toward closing. Then a neighboring condominium board or homeowners association raises objections, makes demands, or declines to sign off on something the buyer wants. The transaction slows down or falls apart entirely, and the seller's attorney files a complaint alleging tortious interference.

It is a pattern New York courts see regularly. And in most cases, the tortious interference claim does not survive.

Understanding why requires a close look at what the claim actually demands, and how far short most real estate disputes fall from meeting it.

Two Different Claims, One Common Mistake

New York recognizes two related but distinct tortious interference theories. The first is tortious interference with contract, which applies when a valid contract exists and a third party intentionally causes one party to breach it. The second is tortious interference with business relations, which applies when no contract exists and a third party uses wrongful means to disrupt a business relationship.

Plaintiffs in real estate disputes frequently plead one when they mean the other, or plead both without appreciating the difference. That matters because the elements of each are specific and the standards are different.

For tortious interference with contract, the plaintiff must show a valid contract, the defendant's knowledge of it, the defendant's intentional and improper procurement of a breach, and actual damages. The critical word is breach. If the underlying contract was ultimately performed, meaning the transaction closed, there is no breach and the claim fails. Courts have been consistent on this point. A contract that was consummated is not a breached contract, regardless of how much difficulty the defendant caused along the way.

For tortious interference with business relations, where no breach is required, the standard is harder in a different way. The plaintiff must show that the defendant acted solely out of malice or used improper or illegal means that amounted to a crime or an independent tort. Good faith assertion of legal rights, even aggressive assertion, does not meet that standard. Negotiating hard, refusing to waive contractual rights, demanding consideration before signing a release, or declining to accommodate a buyer's requests are not tortious acts. They are ordinary exercises of legal and economic self-interest.

Where Condo and HOA Boards Fit In

Boards occupy a particular position in these disputes. When a buyer of adjacent property asks a board to waive recorded easements, sign off on use changes, or release restrictions that run with the land, the board is not interfering in the transaction by declining or by attaching conditions. It is exercising rights that exist independently of the transaction.

The Business Judgment Rule reinforces this protection. Under the framework established in Levandusky v. One Fifth Avenue Apartment Corp., courts defer to board decisions made in good faith, within the scope of the board's authority, and for the legitimate purposes of the community. A board that declines to waive legal rights on behalf of its unit owners without appropriate consideration is not acting maliciously. It is doing its job.

That deference is not absolute. A board that acts arbitrarily, in bad faith, or with the specific purpose of harming a neighbor rather than protecting its community can lose that protection. But bad faith requires more than a plaintiff's allegation that the board's motives were improper. It requires evidence, and that evidence is rarely there.

The Practical Reality

Tortious interference claims against boards tend to be filed early and dismissed later. They consume time, generate legal fees on both sides, and usually resolve in the board's favor once the facts are examined. That does not mean boards are immune from litigation, and it does not mean every board decision is protected. But it does mean that a board acting in good faith to assert the legal rights of its community, or simply declining to give something away without receiving consideration in return, is on firm legal ground.

For property owners and sellers on the other side of these disputes, the lesson is that litigation is not always the fastest path to a resolution. A board that is approached with a genuine offer of consideration, addressing the concerns it has, will often reach an agreement that a lawsuit cannot force.
