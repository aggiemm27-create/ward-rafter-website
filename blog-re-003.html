Who Pays When the Wall Falls Apart?

Shared Retaining Walls and Bulkheads Under New York Law

New York is dense with shared property lines, and along many of them sit walls, fences, bulkheads, and retaining structures that belong to both neighbors at once. When those structures fall apart, the question of who pays for repairs is not always a friendly one. And when one owner steps up to make necessary repairs while the other does nothing, a legal dispute is often not far behind.

The law in New York on this issue is more developed than most property owners realize, and the tools available to the owner who paid are real.

The Administrative Code Obligation

New York City's Administrative Code provides a starting point that many property owners do not know about. Section 28-305.1.1 imposes a joint maintenance obligation on the owners of adjacent properties for retaining walls, partition fences, and other site structures located along a common lot line. Each owner is responsible for one-half of the costs of maintaining and repairing those structures. Courts have applied this provision to require contribution even when one owner had no role in selecting the contractor, approving the scope of work, or agreeing to the price.

The provision does not require a signed contract between the neighbors. It does not require the reluctant owner to have participated in the decision to make repairs. The obligation flows from ownership of the property, not from any agreement between the parties.

Retaining Walls and Bulkheads

The Administrative Code provision covers retaining walls expressly. A retaining wall is defined under NYC Admin. Code Section 28-101.5 as a wall designed to prevent the lateral displacement of soil or other materials.

Bulkheads along waterfront properties present an interesting application of this framework. A bulkhead that runs along a common property line and separates upland from water serves exactly that function: it holds back soil and prevents erosion into the water below. Courts have recognized that a waterfront bulkhead can qualify as a retaining wall under this definition, making the joint maintenance obligation applicable to owners on both sides of the line.

This matters because waterfront bulkheads are expensive to repair, frequently neglected, and often jointly owned by parties whose relationship has deteriorated long before the structure does.

Unjust Enrichment When One Owner Pays

Beyond the Administrative Code, New York's equitable principles provide a second avenue for recovery. When one party is compelled to make a payment that another should have made, the law implies an obligation to reimburse. The Court of Appeals stated it plainly in McDermott v. New York: to prevent unjust enrichment, courts have assumed the duty of placing the obligation where in equity it belongs.

Applied to shared property repairs, the theory works like this. Owner A pays to repair a structure that Owner B was obligated to maintain. Owner B receives a direct benefit, in the form of a repaired structure it did not pay for, at Owner A's expense. Owner B knew the repairs were being made and did nothing to stop them or contribute to the cost. Under those circumstances, Owner B has been unjustly enriched, and a court can require reimbursement.

The knowledge element matters. An owner who can show that the other party was aware of the need for repairs, was presented with the scope of work, had the opportunity to participate, and still refused, is in a stronger position than one who simply paid without any notice to the neighbor.

What This Means Practically

Property owners who find themselves in this situation, having made repairs to a jointly owned structure while a neighbor refuses to contribute, are not without options. The combination of the Administrative Code's express joint maintenance obligation and the equitable principles governing unjust enrichment gives courts a clear basis to require contribution.

Documentation is what makes or breaks these claims. Records of the scope of work, the costs incurred, communications with the neighboring owner, and evidence that the other owner had knowledge of the repairs and their necessity are all critical. Canceled checks, engineer reports, contractor invoices, and correspondence can all become evidence in a contribution claim.

The harder lesson for property owners is the one that comes before any of this. When a shared structure begins to deteriorate, reaching out to the neighboring owner early, documenting that outreach, and making a written demand for contribution before the repairs are even made puts the paying owner in a much stronger legal position than paying first and asking later.
