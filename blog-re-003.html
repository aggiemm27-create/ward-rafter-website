Deed theft complaints filed with the New York Attorney General's Office climbed 240% between 2023 and 2025. The numbers tell part of the story. The rest of it is in the details: forged signatures, phony notarizations, shell company transfers, and homeowners who had no idea their property had changed hands until a stranger showed up at the door. Deed theft is not a new crime in New York, but it is a growing one, and the civil litigation it produces is among the most complex and urgent in real estate law.

How Deed Theft Actually Works

Deed theft schemes typically involve forged signatures, fraudulent notarizations, or deceptive contracts that result in the unlawful transfer of title from an owner to a scammer or shell company. In the most brazen cases, a thief simply forges the owner's signature on a deed and files it with the county clerk. In subtler variations, a homeowner is pressured into signing documents they do not fully understand, believing they are refinancing or entering a loan modification, while actually signing away their title.

Any homeowner can be a victim, but criminals primarily target abandoned or vacant properties, properties in foreclosure, properties with tax or utility liens, and properties where the owner has died and heirs have not yet transferred title. Seniors, immigrants, and homeowners in historically Black neighborhoods have been disproportionately targeted, a pattern city officials have acknowledged contributes to racial wealth gaps and neighborhood destabilization.

The Civil Litigation Problem

Criminal prosecution is one response to deed theft. But for the homeowner who wants their property back, the path runs through civil court.

A forged deed does not simply disappear. Fraudulent conveyances remain in the chain of title until a court declares them void. That declaration comes through a quiet title action, filed in New York Supreme Court, in which the true owner asks the court to establish their ownership and wipe out any competing claims. The process requires clear and convincing evidence that the prior transfer was fraudulent, which may include handwriting analysis, notary records, deed filings, and testimony from the victim.

The complications multiply when a fraudulent deed has already been used as the basis for a mortgage, a sale to a third party, or additional liens. Each of those transactions adds another layer to the litigation and another party who may claim an interest in the property. Unraveling the chain of title in these cases is not quick, and it is not cheap.

What New York Has Done About It

The legal landscape for deed theft has shifted significantly in recent years. In 2023, Governor Hochul signed legislation giving the Attorney General and local district attorneys new authority to act. The Attorney General or a local District Attorney can now file a motion in Supreme Court to void a fraudulent deed. The legislation also empowers law enforcement to stay related legal proceedings, including foreclosures, evictions, and ownership disputes, while a deed theft investigation is ongoing.

Then in April 2026, Mayor Mamdani created New York City's first Office of Deed Theft Prevention by executive order, housed within the Department of Finance. The office flags suspicious property filings, coordinates with law enforcement, conducts public outreach, and improves data-sharing across city agencies.

These are meaningful developments, but they do not eliminate the need for private civil litigation. Government agencies prioritize cases and move at their own pace. A homeowner facing an imminent sale or eviction based on a fraudulent deed often cannot wait for a government investigation to conclude.

What Recovery Looks Like in Court

A successful quiet title action restores the owner's name to the public record and can unwind transactions that flowed from the fraudulent deed. Courts can void not only the original forged transfer but also subsequent mortgages or conveyances where the downstream party had knowledge of the fraud or did not qualify as a bona fide purchaser for value.

New York law includes a 10-year limitations period for certain real property actions, though the applicable period can vary depending on the specific legal theory and procedural posture of the case. Victims who discover a fraudulent transfer years after it occurred may still have viable claims, but timing matters and delay can complicate recovery.

Early Warning Signs

Deed theft often goes undetected for months or years. Homeowners who stop receiving property tax bills, notice unfamiliar names in public property records, receive mail addressed to strangers, or are contacted by buyers or lenders about a property they never sold should treat those signs seriously. Monitoring property records, particularly for owners of vacant or inherited property, is one of the few practical defenses available before a fraudulent transfer occurs.
