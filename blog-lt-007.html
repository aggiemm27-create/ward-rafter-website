The Freeze Is Final

What Rent Guidelines Board Order #58 Means for Landlords Now

The wait is over. On June 25, 2026, the New York City Rent Guidelines Board voted 7 to 1 to set rent adjustments at 0% for both one-year and two-year lease renewals under Rent Guidelines Board Order #58. This covers renewals for rent-stabilized apartments and lofts commencing between October 1, 2026 and September 30, 2027, and it marks the first time in the board's history that a two-year renewal has been frozen alongside a one-year renewal.

The Vote Is In

For landlords who have been tracking this since the preliminary range came out last spring, the final number landed at the bottom of that range. There is no increase to apply on renewal leases that start in this window, full stop. The vote followed months of public hearings, written submissions, and a contested confirmation process for the board's own membership.

What Order #58 Actually Covers

Order #58 applies only to lease renewals for rent-stabilized units. It does not touch new leases signed for vacant apartments, which continue to be set at market rate. It also does not roll back rents already in effect. If a tenant's current lease commenced before October 1, 2026, the prior order, Order #57, still governs that lease until it comes up for renewal.

Order #57 set increases of 3% for one-year renewals and 4.5% for two-year renewals. Landlords managing a portfolio with a mix of lease start dates will need two different orders in play at once this year, depending on when each individual lease commenced.

How This Compares to Past Years

Prior guideline years typically resulted in some increase, even if a modest one. A one-year freeze on its own is not unprecedented in New York City, but pairing it with a 0% increase on two-year renewals is new territory. Owners who have built their budgets around even a small annual bump on renewals will need to adjust those projections for the coming guideline year, particularly if a meaningful share of their units are due for two-year renewals rather than one-year terms.

A Contested Process

The vote did not happen quietly. Mayor Zohran Mamdani appointed six of the board's nine members earlier in 2026 after campaigning on a rent freeze, giving him a working majority heading into the final vote. On the morning of the vote, owner representative Christina Smyth resigned from the board, stating publicly that she would not put her name on what she called a predetermined outcome, and that the board's own research showed operating costs for rent-stabilized buildings rising faster than inflation. Her resignation reduced the board from nine members to eight before the final tally.

None of this changes the legal effect of Order #58. It is adopted and in force for the 2026-27 guideline year. But landlords should understand the context, since continued friction around the board's process would not be surprising.

What Landlords Should Do Now

The freeze does not suspend a landlord's other obligations under New York's rent stabilization framework. Renewal leases still need to be offered on the standard timeline, generally between 90 and 150 days before the current lease expires. DHCR registration requirements remain in effect. Good Cause Eviction Law notice requirements continue to apply where relevant. None of these obligations pause just because the rent number is zero.

Landlords should also confirm which order applies to which unit before sending renewal offers. Applying the wrong percentage, even by mistake, can create an overcharge issue that follows a building for years. With two different orders now governing units depending on lease start date, this is an easy place for an error to slip in during a high-volume renewal season.

Finally, landlords planning capital expenditures or major repairs for the coming year should factor the freeze into their budgeting now rather than waiting until October, since the lack of a rent increase on the renewal side does not change rising costs on the operating side.
