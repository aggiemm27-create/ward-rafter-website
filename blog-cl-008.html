A $6,500 Gap and a Familiar Dispute

In February 2023, a State Farm policyholder's 2018 Audi Q7 was damaged in an accident. She brought the car to Prospect Auto Sales & Repairs. Like many shop customers, she signed three documents: a repair authorization, a form letting the shop negotiate repair costs with State Farm on her behalf, and an assignment of her claim against State Farm for any unpaid repair costs.

The repair came to $19,736.60. State Farm paid $13,188.22. The shop sued State Farm for the $6,548.38 difference, as the policyholder's assignee, for breach of contract.

Why the Trial Court Threw the Case Out

The complaint alleged that State Farm had violated Insurance Law § 2601 and the Department of Financial Services regulation governing auto physical damage claims, 11 NYCRR 216.7. Among other things, that regulation requires an insurer to make "a good faith offer of settlement, sufficient to repair the vehicle to its condition immediately prior to the loss," and to conduct negotiations "in good faith, with the basic goal of promptly arriving at an agreed price."

The Putnam County Supreme Court granted State Farm summary judgment. Its reasoning was that neither § 2601 nor the regulation gives a private party the right to sue for a violation.

Why the Appellate Division Reversed

On September 23, 2026, the Appellate Division, Second Department, reversed and reinstated the case.

The court's first point was about what the shop actually sued for. Read fairly, the complaint was not a lawsuit to enforce the Insurance Law or the regulation. It was a breach of contract claim, and the alleged regulatory violations were part of how the shop said the contract was breached. Whether a private right of action exists under those provisions did not decide the case.

That follows the same court's ruling in M.V.B. Collision v. State Farm earlier this year, which we wrote about here. Together, the two decisions make clear that a shop suing as an assignee can rely on the claims-handling regulations to support a contract claim.

The Survey State Farm Never Produced

The more practical part of the decision is about evidence.

State Farm argued it paid in full because its payment was based on its estimate, which reflected the prevailing labor rate as determined by a survey. The court called that argument "wholly conclusory and unsubstantiated." The reason was simple. State Farm never furnished the survey it said it relied on.

State Farm also submitted an affidavit from one of its representatives. The court found the affidavit was not based on the representative's personal knowledge and relied on "hearsay within hearsay." It could not carry State Farm's burden.

Because State Farm failed to make its initial showing, the court held the motion had to be denied "regardless of the sufficiency of the opposing papers." In New York, a party moving for summary judgment has to prove its own case first. If it does not, the other side does not have to prove anything to defeat the motion.

What This Means for Collision Repair Shops

Insurers often justify their labor rates by pointing to a survey of what local shops charge. Shops are rarely shown the data behind that number. This decision does not say any particular survey is flawed. But it does say that an insurer cannot win summary judgment by describing a survey it never puts in the record.

The decision also shows how much the paperwork matters. The shop's right to sue rested on a signed assignment and a designated representative authorization. Those documents are what allowed it to bring the claim against State Farm directly.

Courts also look closely at who is swearing to what. An insurer's affidavit has to come from someone with real knowledge of the facts, or it has to be backed by proper business records. A claims representative repeating what others told them will not do.

This is not a final win. The decision sends the case back for further proceedings, and the shop still has to prove that State Farm underpaid.

For shops in Staten Island, Brooklyn, Queens, and Long Island, the Second Department's rulings are binding precedent in their trial courts.
