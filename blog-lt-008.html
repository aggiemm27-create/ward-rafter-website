The Freeze Took Effect on Schedule

On June 25, 2026, the NYC Rent Guidelines Board adopted Order #58. It sets a 0% increase for both one-year and two-year renewal leases on rent-stabilized apartments for leases starting between October 1, 2026 and September 30, 2027. It covers roughly one million units across the city.

That order is now in force. Leases and renewals beginning October 1 or later carry no increase while the legal challenge moves forward.

Who Sued and What They Claim

On July 22, 2026, seven landlord entities filed a combined Article 78 petition and declaratory judgment action against the Rent Guidelines Board. The petitioners describe themselves as small, family-owned or self-managed owners with rent-stabilized buildings in Staten Island, Queens, the Bronx, and Brooklyn. They are represented by Randy Mastro, a former first deputy mayor.

The case was filed in Richmond County Supreme Court. In August, it was transferred to Manhattan, where Justice Brendan Lantry is now overseeing it.

At the heart of the petition is a claim that the outcome was decided before the board ever met. The landlords call the vote a "sham process." They argue that the mayor campaigned on a freeze and then appointed board members who were expected to deliver one. They also point to the resignation letter of a board member who wrote that the hearings and data would not change the result.

The petition goes further and questions whether the board acted independently at all. According to the landlords, city offices helped mobilize tenant turnout and advocated for a freeze. In their view, that undercut the board's role as an independent body.

The landlords also argue that the board ignored its own numbers. They allege the board's research showed operating costs rising 5.3%. They also allege its staff estimated that increases of roughly 3.4% to 4.8% were needed just to keep net operating income level. A 0% increase, they say, cannot be squared with that data.

These are allegations. The city disputes them, and tenant advocacy groups have also asked the court to dismiss the case.

What the Judge Has Done So Far

Article 78 challenges to agency decisions are usually hard to win. Courts generally defer to an agency if its decision has a rational basis, and they rarely let petitioners dig into how a decision was made. That is why the early rulings here have drawn attention.

On September 16, Justice Lantry ordered the city to produce written communications between the Mayor's Office, including the mayor himself, and Rent Guidelines Board personnel from January 1 through the June 25 vote. He wrote that the allegations "have raised significant concern to this court." Reports later indicated that the discovery reaches the mayor's work and personal phones.

The city missed its first production deadline. The judge then set a short deadline for screenshots and gave the city 30 days to turn over complete records.

The landlords did not get everything they asked for. They wanted the 2025 increases of 3% and 4.5% to stay in place while the case is pending. Justice Lantry declined, saying he did not believe the court had the authority to impose those increases. He has said he expects to decide by the end of 2026 whether the freeze was lawfully adopted and whether the process has to start over.

Why This Matters for Rent-Stabilized Owners

Even a win for the landlords would not automatically produce a rent increase. The petition asks the court to vacate Order #58 and send the matter back to the board for a new determination. A new vote, by the same board, would follow its own process. And it is not yet clear what would happen to leases signed at 0% in the meantime.

For owners, that leaves two separate timelines. Order #58 governs renewals now. The lawsuit could change the picture later, but not on any fixed date.

Owners with portfolios that span lease start dates are also working under two orders at once. Leases that began before October 1 remain under Order #57's 3% and 4.5% guidelines. Leases beginning on or after October 1 fall under Order #58. Mixing them up can create overcharge exposure that follows a building for years.

What to Watch Next

The next milestones are the city's complete document production and the judge's ruling on the merits, expected before the year is out. Any decision is likely to be appealed by whichever side loses.
