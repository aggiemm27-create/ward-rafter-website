When a Foreclosure Makes Sense, and When It Does Not

A Guide for Condo and HOA Boards

Unpaid common charges and assessments are a chronic problem for condominium and HOA boards in New York. When a unit owner falls months or years behind and every other collection effort has failed, boards sometimes ask whether foreclosure is an option. The answer is yes -- but whether it is the right option depends on a set of practical and legal factors that boards need to understand before going down that road.

The Legal Foundation

Under New York's Condominium Act, a board has the right to file a lien against a unit for unpaid common charges. That lien can, under certain circumstances, be foreclosed in the same manner as a mortgage. For HOAs, a parallel framework exists under New York Real Property Law. The legal authority to foreclose is real. The question is whether exercising it actually recovers money for the community, or simply costs more than it returns.

New Rule: The 90-Day Notice Requirement

Before any board considers foreclosure, it must now comply with a significant procedural change that took effect October 16, 2025. Under Assembly Bill A3470, signed by Governor Hochul, condominium boards and HOAs are required to provide written notice to a unit owner at least 90 days before commencing a foreclosure action to enforce a lien for unpaid common charges, assessments, fines, or fees. The notice must be in 14-point type, state the board's intent to foreclose, identify the property address, and state the exact amount owed. It must also be sent to any other address of record for the owner.

This requirement mirrors the pre-foreclosure notice rules that already apply to residential mortgage lenders. The goal is to give owners a final opportunity to resolve the delinquency before litigation begins. Boards that skip this step or get the notice wrong risk having their foreclosure action dismissed at the outset.

When Foreclosure Can Make Sense

Foreclosure is most worth considering when the arrears are substantial, the unit has real equity, and other collection tools have been exhausted. If a unit owner owes $30,000 in unpaid common charges and the unit is worth $400,000 with a modest first mortgage, the board has a meaningful lien position. A successful foreclosure in that scenario can result in actual recovery.

Boards should also consider foreclosure when a unit owner is unresponsive, has ignored every demand letter and payment plan offer, and shows no sign of resolving the debt. Civil Court and Small Claims judgments are only worth what you can collect on them. When a unit is the only asset of value, a lien and foreclosure may be the most direct path to recovery.

When Foreclosure Does Not Make Sense

The honest reality is that foreclosure is expensive, slow, and unpredictable. Legal fees alone can run into the tens of thousands of dollars before a case is resolved. The process in New York is judicial, meaning it goes through the court system, and can take two years or more from filing to sale. During that time, the board is fronting legal fees with no guarantee of full recovery.

The lien priority question makes this more complicated. Under the Condominium Act, a board's lien for common charges is subordinate to a recorded first mortgage. That means if the unit is sold at foreclosure, the first mortgage lender gets paid before the board sees a dollar. If the unit has little equity after the first mortgage is satisfied, the board may recover nothing despite spending years and significant legal fees pursuing the case.

There is also the question of what happens to the unit itself. If the board acquires the unit at a foreclosure sale, it becomes responsible for carrying costs, maintenance, and eventual resale. That is an outcome most boards are not prepared for and should think through carefully before proceeding.

The Realistic Middle Ground

In practice, foreclosure works best as a credible threat rather than an automatic response. A well-drafted lien, followed by the required 90-day notice and a demand from counsel, often produces a payment arrangement or lump-sum settlement without ever reaching the courthouse. Unit owners who understand that a board is willing and able to foreclose tend to resolve their arrears more quickly than those who believe the board will do nothing.

Before authorizing a foreclosure action, boards should ask their attorney for a realistic assessment of the unit's equity position, the likely cost of the proceeding, and the probability of actual recovery. That analysis should drive the decision, not frustration with a particular owner or a desire to make an example of someone.

A Note on Both Common Charges and Assessments

The foreclosure framework applies to both unpaid common charges in condominiums and unpaid assessments in homeowner associations. The procedural steps differ slightly depending on the entity's structure and governing documents, but the core question is the same: is there enough equity in the unit to justify the cost and time of pursuing the action?
